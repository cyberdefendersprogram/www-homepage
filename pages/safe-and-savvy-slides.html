Slide 8 of 30. All four cards come from fremontpolice.gov/crime-prevention/scams-fraud-financial-crimes. (1) Fremont PD warns that criminals fake caller ID, including the non-emergency line (510) 790-6800; officers never call from it to request information or solicit money; if you get such a call, hang up. They may also use a real officer’s name and claim a missed subpoena, a ticket fine or a tax payment. (2) Neither Fremont PD nor any US government agency will call and demand you send money immediately for the safety of a family member or yourself; call family members first and confirm they are OK; the scammer wants you to react out of emotion. (3) Disturbing-image texts that include a name, address or phone number: that information is generally public or from data breaches; truly specific details (the time you leave for work, a regular sports event) would imply someone is watching. (4) Work-at-home mystery-shopper scams: an official-looking check, instructions to buy gift cards and read out the PIN numbers; the check bounces and the victim loses thousands. Ask for Zoom reactions: who has had a call or text like this? Teen-specific guidance on bullying and AI-faked images comes from ConnectSafely and NCMEC.

Slide 9 of 30. Read each scenario aloud (play A in a worried voice). Ask for A, B, C or D in the chat for each (or launch a Zoom poll if enabled), then reveal: all four are scams. A = AI voice clone. B = Fremont PD documents spoofed caller ID that can even show the department’s own non-emergency number, with claims of a missed subpoena or ticket fine. C = a familiar account that has been taken over; a login code is the key to the account, so never read one out, even to a friend (Discord’s own security page documents malicious downloads, QR codes promising free Nitro and look-alike login sites; it does not specifically cover hijacked friend accounts, so present C as general account-takeover advice). D = the fake-check job scam documented by Fremont PD and the FTC: never pay anyone to get a job. Segue: what is real proof? Something only your family knows.

The Family Password. IN CHAT. A shared secret that proves it’s really them. Pick it together. Everyone in the family, including grandparents who may be called. Make it random. Three unrelated words, like purple-tractor-waffle. Keep it offline. Never typed, texted or posted. Say it out loud only. Ask, don’t offer. The caller must say it. If they can’t, hang up and call back. Chat (2 min): how will you pitch this to your teen? Skip pet names, birthdays and anything on social media.

Slide 10 of 30. The centerpiece. No tech needed, free, five minutes; works for voice clones, spoofed numbers and “I’m at school, send someone to pick me up” texts. How a cheat reaches Dad’s accounts. 1. Lure. “Free Robux” or an “undetected” cheat tool on YouTube, Discord or forums. 2. Run. Your teen runs the file on the family PC. It looks like the real tool. 3. Steal. Saved passwords, login cookies, payment info and crypto wallets from the browser. 4. Drain. Anyone logged in on that browser, including Dad’s email, shopping or bank, is exposed. Why two-step login isn’t enough: stolen login cookies can let attackers in without a password. Sign out of all sessions after an infection.

Group chats and cyberbullying. Pew (2022): 46% of US teens have experienced at least one type of cyberbullying.. Don’t respond or retaliate. A reaction is often what the bully wants, and hitting back can escalate it. Save the evidence. Screenshot messages, usernames and dates. Never keep explicit images. Block and report. Use the app’s block and report tools. It may not end it, but it removes the temptation. Tell a trusted adult. For threats of harm, involve the school and local police. Parents: listen first. You’re lucky if they ask for help. Respond thoughtfully, not fast, and banning the app rarely fixes it.. Group chats: bystanders matter. Don’t forward a humiliating screenshot. Support the target, report it, tell a school adult.

Slide 17 of 30. Verified against ConnectSafely’s Parent’s Guide to Cyberbullying (updated Jul 26, 2025). Steps for teens: it is not your fault; do not respond or retaliate (retaliating can turn one mean act into a chain reaction); save the evidence; block and report (it probably will not end it, but you will be less tempted to respond); get help; for threats of physical harm call local police with a parent and report to the school; if you see it, do not pass along a mean message. Parent points: you are lucky if your child asks for help, since most do not tell; respond thoughtfully, not fast, because acting publicly can worsen the marginalization; what victims say helps most is being truly listened to; solutions matter more than punishment; banning an app or taking the phone does not solve it, because the interaction continues and a ban can leave the teen left out. Kids may call it drama, so avoid leading with the word cyberbullying. Numbers on ConnectSafely’s page: Pew 2022 found 46% of US teens had experienced at least one type of cyberbullying (name-calling 32%, false rumors 22%, unwanted explicit images 17%, explicit images shared without consent 7%); NCES found about 19% of students 12 to 18 were bullied in 2021-22, and 22% of those say it happened online or by text. The group-chat line (leave, do not forward) is a practical addition; ConnectSafely advises not passing along mean messages. Also see connectsafely.org/Sextortion/.

AI-faked images and blackmail. Tell your teen beforehand: a fake still causes real harm, and the target deserves help, not blame.. 1. Don’t share or send more. No forwarding. Never send more photos to a blackmailer. 2. Tell an adult, no blame. Thank your teen for telling you. Stay calm. 3. Report it. To the app, and to NCMEC’s CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org). 4. Get it removed. takeitdown.ncmec.org: free, for under 18, parents can start it. If someone demands money: don’t pay or send more. Keep the threats and account names (not the image), then report.

Let a manager remember for you. ACTION #2. How parents keep track of passwords. NIST survey of 265 parents; multiple answers allowed. Only 12% change passwords by account type; 53% only when needed.. One unique password per account. NIST found parents have fewer passwords than accounts, so reuse creeps in.. One strong master password. Add 2-step login to the manager itself. It is the only one to remember.. Autofill is a fake-site alarm. A manager won’t fill a login on a look-alike site, a useful warning sign.. Apple Passwords (iCloud), Google Password Manager, 1Password, Bitwarden: any works. Pick one this month.

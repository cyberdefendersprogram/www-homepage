The Family Password. IN CHAT. A shared secret that proves it’s really them. 1. Pick it together. Everyone in the family, including grandparents who may be called. 2. Make it random. Three unrelated words, like purple-tractor-waffle. 3. Keep it offline. Never typed, texted or posted. Say it out loud only. 4. Ask, don’t offer. The caller must say it. If they can’t, hang up and call back. Chat (2 min): how will you pitch this to your teen? Skip pet names, birthdays and anything on social media.

Slide 13 of 39. The centerpiece. No tech needed, free, five minutes; works for voice clones, spoofed numbers and “I’m at school, send someone to pick me up” texts. Bad words: pet names, street or school names, birthdays, anything on Instagram or TikTok. Good: three random words or a silly inside joke never posted. Do not post your real word in the chat. How a cheat reaches Dad’s accounts. 1. Lure. “Free Robux” or an “undetected” cheat tool on YouTube, Discord or forums. 2. Run. Your teen runs the file on the family PC. Group chats and cyberbullying. Pew (2022): 46% of US teens have experienced at least one type of cyberbullying.. Don’t respond or retaliate. A reaction is often what the bully wants, and hitting back can escalate it. Save the evidence. Screenshot messages, usernames and dates. Never keep explicit images. Block and report. Use the app’s block and report tools. It may not end it, but it removes the temptation. Tell a trusted adult. For threats of harm, involve the school and local police. Parents: listen first. Slide 21 of 39. Verified against ConnectSafely’s Parent’s Guide to Cyberbullying (updated Jul 26, 2025). Steps for teens: it is not your fault; do not respond or retaliate (retaliating can turn one mean act into a chain reaction); save the evidence; block and report (it probably will not end it, but you will be less tempted to respond); get help; for threats of physical harm call local police with a parent and report to the school; if you see it, do not pass along a mean message. Parent points: you are lucky if your child asks for help, since most do not tell; respond thoughtfully, not fast, because acting publicly can worsen the marginalization; what victims say helps most is being truly listened to; solutions matter more than punishment; banning an app or taking the phone does not solve it, because the interaction continues and a ban can leave the teen left out. Kids may call it drama, so avoid leading with the word cyberbullying. Numbers on ConnectSafely’s page: Pew 2022 found 46% of US teens had experienced at least one type of cyberbullying (name-calling 32%, false rumors 22%, unwanted explicit images 17%, explicit images shared without consent 7%); NCES found about 19% of students 12 to 18 were bullied in 2021-22, and 22% of those say it happened online or by text. The group-chat line (leave, do not forward) is a practical addition; ConnectSafely advises not passing along mean messages. Also see connectsafely.org/Sextortion/.

AI-faked images and blackmail. Tell your teen beforehand: a fake still causes real harm, and the target deserves help, not blame.. 1. Don’t share or send more. No forwarding. Never send more photos to a blackmailer. 2. Tell an adult, no blame. Thank your teen for telling you. Stay calm. 3. Report it. To the app, and to NCMEC’s CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org). 4. Get it removed. takeitdown.ncmec.org: free, for under 18, parents can start it. If someone demands money: don’t pay or send more. Keep the threats and account names (not the image), then report.

Slide 26 of 39. Sensitive topic: keep your tone calm and matter-of-fact, no graphic detail.
